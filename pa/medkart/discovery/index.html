Opportunity statement: Explore whether an in-store transaction can become a useful, consent-led digital record that helps customers understand savings, retain purchase information and manage future needs across store and app.

1. Executive summary

This exercise began with a practical question: how does a product and technology team contribute inside a retail pharmacy business? An exploratory conversation with a Medkart product professional indicated that the team works across retail operations and consumer-facing applications. That prompted field visits to two Medkart stores and approximately five informal interactions with two store representatives.

The representatives demonstrated billing, inventory lookup, customer relationship management and daily sales reporting. They appeared comfortable with the existing system and did not identify an immediate feature request. The responsible product response is therefore not to declare the current workflow broken. Instead, this brief defines a bounded hypothesis around continuity between the in-store purchase and Medkart’s customer app.

The proposed concept is a digital purchase and savings record linked to a customer’s membership contact number. It would surface a pharmacist-verified transaction in the app, allow the customer to assign it to a family profile, show savings transparently and request refill communication with explicit consent. Whether any part of this already exists internally must be verified before product development.

3. Discovery inputs

3.1 Field interactions

  • Two Medkart stores near the candidate’s hostel.
  • Two representatives responsible for customer service and day-to-day store operations; exact internal titles were not collected.
  • Approximately five informal interactions around 15 July 2026.
  • A representative walkthrough lasted approximately 40 minutes.
  • No photographs, recordings or contemporaneous notes were collected; the account is retrospective.

3.2 Public product baseline

As of 19 July 2026, Medkart’s public app listing describes prescription upload, branded-versus-generic comparison, order tracking, automatic refill reminders, family profiles and store discovery. Therefore, refill reminders alone are not a defensible new-feature proposal. The exercise instead examines a possible handoff between an in-store bill and the existing customer app.

5. Product hypothesis

5.1 Hypothesized user need

An in-store customer may benefit from carrying the value of a physical transaction into the app: a durable bill, an understandable savings record, family-level organization and a controlled path to future action. This need has not yet been validated with customers.

5.2 Proposed experience

  1. The representative identifies the member using the customer’s contact number.
  2. The retail system completes a pharmacist-verified transaction.
  3. With appropriate account matching and consent, the transaction becomes visible in the customer’s app.
  4. The app shows purchased items, store, date, amount paid, digital invoice and an explainable savings summary.
  5. The customer assigns the purchase to themselves or an authorized family profile.
  6. The customer explicitly chooses whether and how to receive future refill communication.
  7. The customer can reorder, contact a store or reach pharmacist support without the feature changing medical instructions.

6. Scope boundaries

In scope for the exercise

  • Linking an eligible in-store transaction to an authenticated customer account.
  • Digital invoice and purchase summary.
  • Savings explanation with a defined comparison method.
  • Assignment to an authorized family profile.
  • Explicit, editable refill-communication preferences.
  • Support, error, duplicate-account and unmatched-transaction states.

Out of scope

  • Diagnosis, dosage advice or clinical decision support.
  • Automatic medicine substitution or alteration of a prescription.
  • Unverified claims that a medicine has been consumed.
  • A redesign of Medkart’s retail billing or inventory platform.
  • A full loyalty-program redesign.
  • Assuming that the proposed capability does not already exist internally.

8. Safety, privacy and trust requirements

  • Do not expose purchase information to another family member without clear authorization.
  • Do not send reminders until the customer has chosen the channel and granted valid consent.
  • Use only pharmacist-verified transaction data; do not infer dosage or adherence.
  • Explain how savings are calculated and avoid misleading comparisons.
  • Support account mismatch, recycled phone number, shared family number and customer-data deletion scenarios.
  • Maintain an audit trail for account linking, preference changes and outbound communication.

9. Research required before a PRD is approved

The next phase should test the problem before refining the solution. A small directional study can begin with 5–8 in-store customers, 3–5 store representatives, 1–2 area managers and relevant product, CRM, engineering and compliance stakeholders.

  • What do customers do with printed and digital pharmacy bills after purchase?
  • Do customers understand the membership benefit and the savings they received?
  • Would customers use in-store history inside the app, and for which task?
  • How are shared family contact numbers and family profiles handled today?
  • When and how is reminder consent collected, updated and withdrawn?
  • Which billing, CRM and app identifiers can safely match a transaction to an account?
  • What store exceptions occur when a member cannot be found or uses another person’s number?
  • How should savings be calculated, displayed and audited?

10. Decision gate and next artifacts

Proceed to detailed design only if research shows that customers value the store-to-app record, internal systems can support reliable account matching, and legal/privacy review accepts the proposed consent and family-access model.

  • Customer, store representative and area manager research questionnaire.
  • Current-state and proposed-state workflow diagrams.
  • Product Requirements Document with user stories and acceptance criteria.
  • Wireframes for purchase history, savings, family assignment and reminder consent.
  • Analytics and event-tracking plan.
  • QA plan, test cases, bug report and release checklist.
  • Store SOP, customer user guide and escalation process.

Research limitations

  • The store sample was small, geographically convenient and limited to experienced representatives.
  • Interactions were informal and were not recorded through notes, audio, photographs or a structured interview guide.
  • The representatives’ internal role titles, the membership duration and the digital-bill channel remain unverified.
  • No customers, newer representatives, area managers, product managers, engineers or compliance stakeholders have yet been interviewed for this concept.
  • Public app descriptions can change and may omit capabilities that already exist.
  • No access was available to Medkart’s retail software, CRM data model, consent records or app architecture beyond the store walkthrough.
  • The proposed experience is therefore a validation-ready hypothesis, not a product recommendation ready for implementation.
