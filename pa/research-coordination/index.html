Ancient Intelligence Lab

Problem

First-year students had no structured, low-stakes space to build confidence, creative expression and courage before college pressure fully set in — the program the founder wished they'd had.

Role

Founder and primary facilitator — designed the program, ran intuition-based selection, facilitated every session across three cohorts, and personally wrote each convocation letter.

Complete for this cycle: 70+ applicants, 14 offers, 7 graduates across 3 cohorts. Evidence is real but largely qualitative — no baseline or post-program measurement was run.

Designing and running a small-cohort development experience for first-year students

Full name / tagline: Ancient Intelligence Labs — “Find Yourself Before the World Finds You” Role: Founder and primary facilitator Institution: ADIT, New Vallabh Vidyanagar (invite-only cohorts) Period: Ran across the academic year, across 3 cohorts

Evidence note: Where a fact comes from a live artifact (the program’s landing page, an offer letter, participant testimonials, a recognition letter) it’s stated directly. Where a detail is remembered rather than pulled from a record, it’s labeled Recalled — approximate, not verified against records.

1. Why I built this

“It was something I wished I got in my first year.” The program exists to give first-year students an experience the founder didn’t have access to at that stage — a structured but informal space to build confidence, creative expression and courage before the pressures of college fully set in.

Cohorts: 3, targeted at 5 unique students per cohort. Sessions: 3–5 per cohort, roughly 2 hours each (per the program’s own FAQ: “We meet for a 2-hour session once or twice a week. Each module usually needs 1–2 sessions.”)

3. How students found out and applied

Recruitment happened through the WhatsApp groups of all branches at the institution — this was an invite-only, informally-distributed opportunity rather than a broadly advertised one. Applicants filled out a Google Form application, described on the landing page as intentionally “thought-provoking” — designed to “reveal your real self” rather than collect a standard resume.

4. How selection worked

Selection from 70+ applicants down to 14 offers was intuition-based, guided by specific signals of agency rather than a formal scoring rubric:

  • Whether an applicant answered optional questions (a signal of extra effort)
  • Whether they attached proof of work — a portfolio, GitHub, or even an Instagram page showing something they’d made or done

The program’s own landing page names the traits it was explicitly looking for: obsessed about something, curious about life, emotional, coachable, learns from mistakes, high energy. These match and reinforce the intuition-based approach described above — they weren’t formally scored, but they were the qualities being read for.

5. The experience — three modules, then convocation

The journey (as described on the program’s own site): Apply → Confidence → Creativity → Courage → Convocation.

A typical session opened with a discussion of what the module’s expected outcome was, moved into open conversation and group discussion, included some structured challenges, and closed once the module’s goal had been reached.

  • Confidence: understanding personal strengths and blind spots, aligning actions with ambitions, taking a first bold step.
  • Creativity: unlocking a personal mode of creative expression and building a sustainable habit around it.
  • Courage: stepping outside a comfort zone and acting decisively. In practice, this often took the shape of a participant organizing an event independently — securing permission, planning, inviting an audience, and presenting. One documented example: a participant organized and presented an entire art-form event in their first month of college, handling everything from college permissions to anchoring and presenting it themselves.
  • Convocation: instead of a certificate, participants graduate with something the program considers more meaningful — a personalized letter recognizing their growth, addressed to their parents.

6. Why it shifted from conversation-heavy to challenge-based

The program initially leaned more heavily on facilitated conversation. Coordinating long discussions became difficult once real scheduling constraints hit — participants had to be pulled together across roughly ten different timetables alongside the facilitator’s own. The program shifted toward practical, challenge-based activities as a result, which had two effects: participants learned by doing rather than only discussing, and progress became less dependent on everyone being available at the same time for extended conversation.

7. Why some participants didn’t finish

Of the 10 who started, 3 did not complete the program. The cause was a mix of scheduling difficulty and participants not showing up for the practical challenges — not a single, isolated reason.

9. Honest limitations

  • Selection was guided partly by founder intuition rather than a documented rubric.
  • A refundable ₹50 deposit was advertised on the landing page but was never actually collected or enforced.
  • The stated Round 1 deadline (21 August 2025) was not the deadline actually followed — real cohort dates are approximate.
  • Original Google Form application data is no longer accessible.
  • Program outcomes were primarily qualitative — no baseline or post-program confidence measurement was run.
  • Public portfolio evidence has been anonymized where it included real participant names.

10. What I’d improve next time

  • Introduce explicit, documented selection criteria instead of relying on intuition alone.
  • Record participant consent systematically, and preserve original application data.
  • Track attendance and challenge completion formally, rather than relying on memory.
  • Design session scheduling around participant timetable availability from the start, rather than adjusting mid-program.
  • Establish a baseline and post-program measurement, even if lightweight, to move beyond purely qualitative outcomes.
  • Maintain a structured, ongoing research repository instead of point-in-time evidence collection.

11. What I actually learned

“How to understand people and use analogies to explain things to them — instead of telling or screaming at them, how to use questions to shape their decisions.” This is the single clearest personal takeaway from running the program directly: the shift from directive instruction to facilitated self-discovery, applied consistently across every module.
